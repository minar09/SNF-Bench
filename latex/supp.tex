\noindent
This supplement records the material supporting the main paper without being
required to follow its argument: the region-partition procedure and its
sensitivity study, complete audit tables for both tracks at every horizon,
measurement coverage, protocols for the two complementary perceptual checks, and
additional qualitative material. The text-conditioned and image-conditioned
tracks are reported here at equal depth. Section numbering is independent of the
main paper.

\section{Region Partition}
\label{sup:masks}

\paragraph{Derivation.}
For every generated sequence we average optical-flow magnitude~\cite{teed2020raft}
over the leading $12\%$ of sampled frame pairs and threshold it by Otsu's
method~\cite{otsu1979threshold} into a dynamic region and its complement. The
static region is then reduced by a fixed morphological erosion and a $4\%$ frame
border discarded; together these form the ignored transition band, so boundary
pixels---where the partition is least certain and flow least reliable---contribute
to neither region. The same procedure applies to both tracks.

\paragraph{Why the estimate is restricted to an early window.}
Deriving a partition from a sequence's entire motion field would be circular,
since accumulated background drift would itself be read as evidence that the
affected region is dynamic. Restricting the estimate to the early window breaks
that circularity in the direction that matters: at the $60$\,s horizon the
partition is fixed from the first $7.1$\,s, before long-horizon drift has
accumulated, and the metrics are evaluated over the remainder.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The partition is obtained automatically rather than by annotation, so no
inter-annotator agreement is reported, and for the image-conditioned track it is
derived per generated sequence rather than once from the shared source image.
The early window is a weaker guarantee than a first-frame annotation: a system
that drifts within the first $12\%$ of its rollout can influence its own
partition. We therefore treat partitions as measured quantities and report the
consequence directly---every measurement is recomputed under systematic erosion
and dilation of the boundary, with the response in Table~\ref{tab:validation}.

\section{Validation Protocol}
\label{sup:validation}

Geometric severity is specified in a common physical unit---the mean pixel
displacement a corruption induces over the frame by the end of the
rollout---and each family's parameter is derived from it using the frame
geometry: a translation of $d$ pixels, a rotation of $d/\bar{r}$ radians, or a
zoom of $1 + d/\bar{r}$, where $\bar{r}$ is the mean distance from the frame
centre. This is what makes the geometric families comparable. A parameter in
degrees is not commensurate with one in pixels: at the evaluation resolution one
degree of rotation induces roughly $2.7$\,px of mean displacement, so families
compared by their native parameters differ in injected severity by more than an
order of magnitude, and a factor can appear non-monotone simply because the
corruption was too weak to clear the clip's own baseline drift.

\section{Signed Drift Attenuation}
\label{sup:dar}

Drift attenuation is stored signed and reported clipped to $[0,1]$. Negative
values are informative rather than anomalous: where local flow opposes the
estimated global field, compensation increases the measured magnitude. We report
their incidence rather than suppressing it.

\section{Complementary Perceptual Checks}
\label{sup:perceptual}

Neither check is aggregated into a factor and neither is a preference study.
Both answer one question: does an independent judge, with no access to our
factors, order the same clip pairs the same way?

\paragraph{Pair selection.}
Pairs are chosen by rule rather than by eye. For each axis, candidate pairs of
systems on a shared prompt are binned by the \emph{relative gap} in the relevant
factor and sampled across three bands ($0.05$--$0.20$, $0.20$--$0.50$,
$0.50$--$1.00$), so agreement is measured over the whole operating range rather
than only where any metric would look good. Twelve pairs are drawn per axis.

\paragraph{Human study.}
Raters answer exactly two forced-choice questions---which video's background
moves more, and in which video the moving content slows or stops more---and are
never asked which is better, since ``better'' conflates the two failures the
benchmark separates. Trial order and left/right presentation are randomised per
rater, choices are resolved back to system identities server-side, and system
names are never shown. Ties are permitted: forcing a choice on an
indistinguishable pair manufactures noise. Twenty-four trials per rater, about
eight minutes, three raters.

\paragraph{Vision--language judge.}
The same pairs, with the same wording, are put to an open-weight
vision--language model~\cite{qwen3vl2025}, following the practice of
MLLM-scored generation benchmarks~\cite{han2025videobench,he2024videoscore} but
restricted to the two axis questions rather than an overall score. Each pair is
asked twice with the clips swapped; a judge answering by position rather than by
content disagrees with itself, so agreement is computed only over
self-consistent trials and the self-consistency rate is reported beside it.

\paragraph{Reporting the human study.}
For the human study we report, per axis, the agreement rate with a binomial
interval, the tie rate, and agreement resolved by metric gap. The last matters:
a factor that agrees with observers only on large gaps is a weaker result than
one that agrees throughout, and pooling hides the difference. The same format
is defined for the vision--language judge, but as set out below its trials did
not meet the precondition for reporting an agreement rate at all.

\paragraph{Outcome of the vision--language check: negative.}
We report this check as a negative result. Before reading any agreement rate we
required the judge to pass two controls. Shown one clip against itself, where
the only correct answer is that the two are the same, it answered correctly on
five of six pairs. Shown a frozen single frame against a real sequence, where
the moving sequence must be chosen and the choice must follow the clip when the
two are swapped, it succeeded on three of six. On the audited pairs it was
self-consistent under swapping on seven of twenty-four, which is what a
three-way choice made at random would produce. An agreement rate computed over
so few self-consistent trials describes the judge's presentation bias rather
than the videos, so we do not report one, and no number from this check enters
any factor, table or figure elsewhere in this work.

We record the outcome because it is informative about the task rather than only
about the model. The distinction the benchmark draws---between a background
that drifts and intended motion that decays---lies below the discrimination
threshold of a current open-weight judge at this scale, even though the same
judge reliably detects that a clip is identical to itself. This is consistent
with the main finding that whole-frame motion scores do not separate these two
failures either, and it is the reason the benchmark rests on mechanistic
validation under injected corruptions rather than on a model's preferences. We
report the protocol and its controls in full so the check can be repeated at a
larger scale; we do not present it as evidence for the factors.
